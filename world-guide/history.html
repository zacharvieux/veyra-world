Veyra's present is shaped by one event more than any other: the Sundering.

Roughly a thousand years ago, the lands between Aldara and Caelora were catastrophically broken. The sea flooded the shattered region, civilizations vanished, the old geography changed, and the Old Gods disappeared from ordinary mortal life.

Everyone agrees that the Sundering happened.

Almost nobody agrees on why.

Solvarran theologians preserve one explanation. Elderwood songs preserve another. Caeloran scholars reconstruct the catastrophe through ruins, magical measurements, and geology. Dwarven archives contain engineering records whose terminology no longer has obvious equivalents. Tsukaran stories remember the Crossing Through the White. The Shattered Isles contain physical evidence that contradicts parts of nearly every surviving tradition.

The result is a world in which history is not merely academic.

History determines:

  • who claims old land;
  • whose religion is considered legitimate;
  • who owns recovered ruins;
  • which nations believe they inherited responsibility for the past; and
  • which truths powerful institutions would rather leave buried.

The Public Shape of Ancient History

Most educated people accept that the pre-Sundering world was older, more religiously diverse, and in some ways more magically sophisticated than the modern one.

Ancient temples to Solenne, Nyvara, Elyra, and Morthain once stood across lands that are now divided among different nations. Numerous lesser gods, local sacred beings, city cults, river traditions, ancestral faiths, and regional religions also existed.

Ancient elves and dwarves are especially associated with surviving pre-Sundering works, but ruins demonstrate that the ancient world was not divided neatly by ancestry. Mixed settlements, trade, shared temples, and structures combining traditions are well documented.

Modern scholars generally refer to the long period before the catastrophe as the Old Age or Pre-Sundering Age rather than pretending they understand its political boundaries precisely.

The Waning in Historical Scholarship

The religious term the Waning is increasingly used by scholars for the centuries before the Sundering when reports of newly appearing gods became rarer and older divine traditions describe a world that felt less saturated with sacred presence than earlier ages.

Most scholars do not agree on what the Waning actually was.

Common theories include:

  • religious decline;
  • natural cycles in magic;
  • changing relationships between mortals and gods;
  • exaggeration in older religious texts; or
  • a genuine metaphysical transition that modern scholarship cannot yet explain.

The important public fact is that the Waning appears to predate the Sundering.

Year 0 — The Sundering

For international scholarship, the Sundering is the dividing point of history.

The catastrophe:

  • separated Aldara and Caelora;
  • created the Shattered Sea;
  • destroyed or drowned a vast central region;
  • left the Shattered Isles as surviving fragments;
  • ruined cities, roads, temples, and institutions;
  • displaced enormous populations; and
  • coincided with the disappearance of the Old Gods from direct mortal life.

Public explanations remain disputed.

Caeloran historians usually describe the Sundering as an ancient magical catastrophe, possibly involving a failed large-scale ritual or magical war.

Elven traditions generally imply that mortal choices played a role but preserve incomplete and guarded accounts.

Dwarven records show that ancient dwarven engineers participated in large cross-cultural projects shortly before the catastrophe, but surviving records do not provide one uncontested explanation.

Solvarran doctrine tends to interpret the ancient world through the later revelation of Aureon and treats many pre-Aureonite traditions with suspicion.

Tsukaran tradition remembers survival through the Crossing Through the White and the extraordinary sacred role of the first Emperor.

In the Shattered Isles, the ruins simply refuse to cooperate with simple explanations.

The Years of Ash and Water, 0–63 SR

The first generations after the Sundering are remembered collectively as the Years of Ash and Water.

This name does not mean that every region experienced the same disaster in the same way. It describes a period of broad instability:

  • coastal flooding;
  • failed harvests;
  • refugee migrations;
  • broken roads;
  • collapsed trade routes;
  • local wars;
  • epidemics;
  • abandoned temples;
  • strange magical events; and
  • widespread uncertainty about whether the catastrophe had actually ended.

Aldaran oral traditions remember villages moving inland and old noble families disappearing entirely.

Caeloran records describe river systems changing course, provincial governments losing contact with one another, and emergency grain movements becoming the foundation of later state institutions.

Dwarven holds became critical suppliers of tools, metal, stonework, and engineered defenses even while struggling to feed their own populations.

Tsukaran histories remember maritime evacuation, survival fleets, storm losses, and the beginning of stricter naval organization.

The Shattered Isles did not yet exist as a coherent inhabited region. Many surviving islands were considered cursed, unstable, or simply too dangerous to settle permanently.

The Age of New Crowns, 64–210 SR

As immediate survival stabilized, new political orders emerged.

This period is remembered differently by every nation, because most modern states prefer to describe their own consolidation as restoration rather than invention.

During these centuries:

  • Solvarran crown-and-church institutions began taking recognizable form;
  • Ardaren noble houses consolidated river valleys and cavalry territories;
  • Valemere's trade cities increasingly coordinated law and commercial standards;
  • Karthain's mixed clans and settled communities developed stronger shared institutions;
  • Tharakai's warrior monarchy and clan systems hardened into a more unified kingdom;
  • Durakhal renewed the political compact among the Holds; and
  • eastern Caeloran regions began rebuilding large-scale administrative and transport systems.

Aureon — approximately 70 SR

The earliest widely accepted Aureonite miracle traditions date from roughly seventy years after the Sundering, placing Aureon's rise about nine centuries before the present.

Aureonite tradition does not describe this as the birth of a new god. It describes the emergence or revelation of a divine truth that humanity had failed to recognize correctly before.

Skeptics disagree.

Believers point to real miracles.

The argument has never been settled.

Varesh — approximately 90 SR

The earliest broadly accepted traditions of Varesh as a major divine figure appear roughly two decades later in northern beastfolk lands.

Tharakan traditions portray Varesh as ancient, primal, and inseparable from beastfolk endurance. Other regions preserve different stories, and Karthain's mixed culture never accepted the strongest Tharakan claims of exclusive beastfolk spiritual destiny.

Again, the miracles are real even where the theology is contested.

The Reconstruction Centuries, 211–499 SR

By the third century after the Sundering, most major populations were no longer living in immediate post-catastrophe survival conditions.

The central historical problem became reconstruction.

Old roads were repaired where possible and abandoned where not. New kingdoms standardized tolls, law, weights, military obligations, and border defenses. Valemerian merchant networks expanded across Aldara. Durakhal's metal and engineering trades became indispensable to northern states. Tsukaran shipbuilding and navigation restored long maritime routes.

Caelora developed differently.

Rather than fragmenting permanently into numerous kingdoms, its major eastern regions slowly built increasingly formal systems of shared stewardship.

The Covenant of Stewardship, 412 SR

In 412 SR, after decades of provincial disputes over grain, water, roads, taxation, and disaster relief, representatives of the major eastern regions signed the Covenant of Stewardship.

The Covenant did not create modern Caelora overnight. It established the principle that legitimacy depended upon maintaining the conditions necessary for ordinary people to live.

The Crown would possess extraordinary authority, but that authority carried enforceable duties.

The earliest version of the Covenant recognized obligations concerning:

  • food reserves;
  • roads and bridges;
  • public safety;
  • courts;
  • emergency medicine; and
  • protection from local rulers who endangered their own populations.

Later reforms expanded those obligations into the modern Common Standard.

This date gives Caelora a state tradition old enough to feel deeply rooted without making the present system unchanged for a thousand years.

The Saltstone Expansion, 500–779 SR

Saltstone was known long before this period, but it was difficult to refine consistently and dangerous to use at industrial scale.

The decisive change came when Caeloran researchers and High Spine engineers developed increasingly reliable methods for:

  • stabilizing refined crystals;
  • routing power through insulated channels;
  • replacing cracked components;
  • measuring output; and
  • standardizing safety procedures.

What had once powered individual devices could now support entire systems.

Cities gained pumps and lighting. Hospitals adopted new equipment. Refrigerated storage improved food distribution. Mines became deeper and safer in some respects. Long-distance communications became faster.

Caelora's central political promise and its technological development began reinforcing each other:

Better infrastructure made the Common Standard possible.
The Common Standard created political pressure for more infrastructure.

This relationship remains fundamental to modern Caelora.

The Rail Century, 780–919 SR

The First Arcrail Trunk — date 781 SR

In 781 SR, the first major interregional Arcrail trunk entered regular service.

Earlier experimental rails existed, but this was the line that proved saltstone-powered continental transport could become ordinary infrastructure rather than a laboratory achievement.

Within a century, Arcrail reshaped Caelora.

It changed:

  • where people could work;
  • how quickly food moved;
  • military response times;
  • access to universities and hospitals;
  • migration;
  • national identity; and
  • expectations of government.

A farmer hundreds of miles from the capital could now reasonably expect national institutions to affect daily life.

Aldaran observers were divided between admiration, alarm, and disbelief.

The Connected Age, 920 SR–Present

The last century has seen Veyra become more connected than at any point since the Sundering.

Arcrail networks spread across Caelora. International postal contracts expanded. Merchant ships became faster and more reliable. Printing became cheaper. Universities shared scholarship. Caeloran clinics accepted foreign patients. Tsukaran navigators mapped safer maritime routes without surrendering the privacy of their homeland.

At the same time, connection has created new conflicts.

Aldaran rulers worry about Caeloran economic influence. Caelora debates how far stewardship obligations extend beyond its own citizens. Tsukara regulates foreign access carefully. Shattered Isles settlements bargain for recognition, protection, or independence.

Modern Veyra is therefore not a world in which old and new are separate.

They are colliding.
